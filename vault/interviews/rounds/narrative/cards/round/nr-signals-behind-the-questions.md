## Q
A loop will ask you fifteen differently-worded behavioral questions. If the interviewer is really filling in a short rubric, what is on it — and why does knowing the list mean you need far fewer prepared answers than questions?

## A
Almost every question is a probe for one of five signals:

- **Scope** — how big was the problem you *chose to see*? Not the task you were handed.
- **Ownership** — did you own the decision and its consequence, or just the execution? The tell is whether the story contains a decision you could have gotten wrong.
- **Judgment under cost** — did you trade something real away, knowingly? An answer with no cost paid is an answer with no judgment in it.
- **Conflict** — can you disagree with a person and still work with them afterwards?
- **Self-correction** — did anything actually change in how you operate, and can you name the mechanism?

Questions map many-to-one onto signals. "Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager", "a time you pushed back on a deadline", and "a time you were the only one who thought something was a bad idea" are **one signal probed three ways**.

So prepare **stories per signal**, not answers per question — and when a new question arrives, identify the signal first and *then* pick the story.
