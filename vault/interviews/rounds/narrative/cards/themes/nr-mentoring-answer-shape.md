## Q
You are telling a story about mentoring someone. What must it contain to show the senior signal, and what are the common ways it goes wrong?

## A
**Must contain:**

- **A concrete starting point.** Not "they were junior" but what they could and could not yet do: "their changes passed review but broke under concurrency, and they did not write tests for failure paths."
- **Your diagnosis of the gap.** Skill, confidence, context or habits? Each needs a different response.
- **What you did instead of fixing it yourself.** Pairing on one change, review comments that explain why, a small owned project with you as backstop.
- **How you checked it stuck.** Their later work, without you in the loop.
- **Their outcome, not yours.** They now own the area, review others, were promoted. The story's hero is the other person.

**How it goes wrong:**

- **The hero version.** "I rewrote their code and showed them how it should be done" is a story about you.
- **Condescension.** Describing the person as a problem, or their old work as bad, costs more than the story earns.
- **No evidence of transfer.** If the only proof is "they thanked me", the interviewer cannot tell whether anything changed.
- **Only one person, ever.** Mention how the same approach became a team habit, if it did.
